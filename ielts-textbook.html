Chapter 1 · Reading

Find the evidence, then answer.

IELTS reading rewards precise evidence. First identify keywords in the question, then scan for the same idea expressed through synonyms. Read the surrounding sentence carefully before deciding.

Passage

Community gardens are sometimes praised mainly for producing food. Yet their social value may be equally important. Shared gardening spaces bring residents into repeated contact, allowing trust to develop gradually. In neighbourhoods where people rarely meet, this regular interaction can create new support networks.

True, False, or Not Given?

1. Food production is the only important benefit of community gardens.
2. Regular meetings in gardens can help residents build trust.
3. Most community gardens receive government funding.

Answers

1. False—the text says social value may be equally important.
2. True—the text directly connects repeated contact with trust.
3. Not Given—funding is never discussed.

Avoid the “same word” trap

A statement can repeat words from the passage and still be false. Match the full meaning, including limits such as some, mainly, or always.

Chapter 2 · Listening

Predict the type of answer.

Before the audio reaches a gap, use grammar and context to predict whether you need a name, number, place, noun, adjective, or action. This reduces the number of possibilities competing for your attention.

Example form

Workshop location: Room ____
Students should bring: a ____
Main activity: analysing ____ data

Predictions: a room number; a singular object; an adjective or field before “data.”

Read as listening practice

“The workshop was originally planned for Room 18, but the larger group means we will meet in Room 30. You do not need a laptop; please bring a notebook. We will spend most of the session analysing environmental data collected near the river.”

Complete the three gaps above.

Answers

30 · notebook · environmental

Watch for corrections

Speakers often mention an old detail before correcting it. Words such as but, actually, instead, and rather than signal that the answer may change.

Chapter 3 · Writing

Describe patterns, then support them.

Academic Task 1: visual information

A strong response selects important features. It does not describe every number. Begin with an overview of the largest patterns, then group related details.

Practice data

A university survey shows bicycle commuting at 18% in 2015, 24% in 2020, and 31% in 2025. Car commuting falls from 52% to 44% to 35% over the same years. Public transport remains near 25%.

Model overview

Overall, cycling became increasingly common while car use declined substantially. By contrast, the proportion using public transport remained broadly stable.

Task 1 structure

Introduction (paraphrase) → Overview (main patterns) → Detail group 1 → Detail group 2

Academic Task 2: essay

Body paragraph

Clear claim → explanation → specific example → connection to the question

Try it

Some universities require all students to study at least one course outside their main subject. Is this a positive or negative development? Write one body paragraph supporting a clear position.

Chapter 4 · Speaking

Extend an answer naturally.

A one-sentence reply rarely demonstrates your full ability. Extend with a reason, example, contrast, or result—but do not memorise a speech that ignores the question.

Question

Do you enjoy studying in libraries?

Short answer

Yes, I do.

Developed answer

Yes, especially when I need to write. At home I’m easily distracted, whereas the library creates a clear boundary between study time and free time. I usually choose a quiet desk and work there for about two hours.

Natural extension choices

Answer + because… · Answer + for example… · Answer + whereas… · Answer + so…

Try it aloud

Would you prefer to live in a large city or a small town? Give an answer, one reason, and one personal example.

  • I answer the exact question.
  • I explain rather than repeat.
  • I use words I can control accurately.
  • I keep speaking naturally after a small mistake.
Final self-check

Measure progress by decisions, not tricks.

Ask whether you can locate evidence, predict answer types, select key trends, organise an argument, and extend a spoken answer. These transferable skills matter more than memorising impressive phrases.

Seven-day cycle

Days 1–2: reading evidence · Day 3: listening prediction · Days 4–5: one Task 1 overview and one Task 2 paragraph · Days 6–7: record and review three speaking answers.
