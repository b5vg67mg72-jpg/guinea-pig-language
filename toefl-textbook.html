Chapter 1 · Reading

Read for structure, not every word.

Academic passages become easier when you track the writer’s purpose. On a first pass, look for the topic, the main claim, and the job of each paragraph. Do not stop at every unfamiliar word.

The three-question reading map
  1. What is the passage mainly explaining or arguing?
  2. What role does each paragraph play?
  3. Which details directly support the main idea?
Practice passage

Urban trees do more than improve a city’s appearance. Their leaves provide shade, which can lower surface temperatures during hot weather. Trees also absorb some airborne pollutants and reduce rainwater runoff by slowing the movement of water into drainage systems. However, these benefits depend on careful planning because unsuitable species may damage pavements or struggle in local conditions.

Try it

Choose the best main idea:

  1. All cities should plant the same tree species.
  2. Urban trees provide several benefits but require thoughtful planning.
  3. Rainwater is the greatest problem in modern cities.
Check

B is best. It includes both halves of the passage: benefits and the need for planning. A contradicts the passage, and C is too narrow.

Vocabulary in context

Before using a dictionary, ask whether the unknown word sounds positive, negative, causal, or contrasting. In the passage, runoff appears after “rainwater” and before “drainage systems,” so it probably refers to water moving across a surface.

Chapter 2 · Listening

Take notes on relationships.

Good notes are not a transcript. Record the speaker’s organisation: claim, reason, example, contrast, and result. Use short forms so your attention stays on the lecture.

Mini-lecture notes

Topic: Why some animals migrate
Reason 1: food changes by season → birds follow insects
Reason 2: reproduction → safer breeding area
Contrast: not all migration = long distance
Example: mountain animals move uphill/downhill

Useful note symbols

→ causes/results in   |   ≠ contrast   |   ex = example   |   + / − = advantage/disadvantage

Try it

Read this once as if you were hearing it: “Researchers once assumed sleep simply allowed the body to rest. More recent evidence suggests that sleep also supports memory. During sleep, the brain appears to strengthen some newly formed connections while reducing less useful ones. This may explain why students often remember material better after sleeping than after studying continuously through the night.”

Write no more than four lines of notes, including the old view, new view, process, and result.

Possible notes

Old: sleep = body rest
New: also supports memory
Process: strengthens useful brain links; reduces others
Result: sleep after study → better recall than all-night study

Chapter 3 · Speaking

Speak from a small, visible plan.

A clear response usually needs fewer ideas, not more. Use a simple structure: position, reason, example, closing connection. Plan with keywords rather than complete sentences.

PREP structure

Point → Reason → Example → Point again

Prompt

Do students learn better alone or in groups?

Keyword plan

Groups / explain ideas / biology project / noticed misunderstanding / therefore active discussion

Model response

I think students often learn better in small groups because explaining an idea reveals what they truly understand. For example, during a biology project, I tried to explain a process to my classmates and noticed that one part of my explanation was unclear. Their questions helped me correct the mistake. Therefore, group discussion can make learning more active and accurate.

Try it

Prepare a four-line keyword plan: Is it better to take a difficult class or an easy class?

  • I answer the question immediately.
  • I develop one main reason.
  • My example is specific.
  • My final sentence connects to the position.
Chapter 4 · Writing

Connect sources instead of listing them.

Integrated academic writing asks you to explain how ideas relate. Your task is not to add personal opinions. Identify the central relationship—agreement, disagreement, qualification, or example—and organise around it.

Reading claim

Online lectures increase access because students can study at a convenient time.

Lecture response

Flexibility helps only when learners have reliable technology and enough self-discipline; otherwise completion may fall.

Relationship sentence

Although the reading presents flexibility as a major advantage of online lectures, the speaker qualifies this claim by arguing that access alone does not guarantee successful completion.

Paragraph pattern

Source A claim → Source B response → precise supporting detail → why the relationship matters

Try it

Write 80–120 words explaining the relationship between the reading and lecture above. Do not include your own opinion.

  • I identify the relationship accurately.
  • I attribute ideas to the reading or speaker.
  • I paraphrase instead of copying.
  • Every detail supports the relationship.
Self-check

How to judge your practice.

For reading and listening, compare meaning rather than exact wording. For speaking and writing, record or save your first attempt, then revise only one weakness at a time: organisation, support, language accuracy, or delivery.

A useful weekly cycle

Day 1: reading structure · Day 2: listening notes · Day 3: speaking response · Day 4: integrated writing · Day 5: repeat the weakest skill with new material.
